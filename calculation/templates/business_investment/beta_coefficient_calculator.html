Interpreting Your Beta ($\beta$):
  • Beta > 1.0: The stock is **more volatile** than the market. It tends to rise and fall by a larger percentage than the market index. (Higher Systematic Risk)
  • Beta $\approx$ 1.0: The stock moves **in line** with the market. If the market rises by 1%, the stock is expected to rise by approximately 1%. (Average Systematic Risk)
  • Beta < 1.0 (but $>$ 0): The stock is **less volatile** than the market. It offers a defensive position during market downturns. (Lower Systematic Risk)
  • Beta $\leq$ 0: The stock moves **opposite** to the market (or has no correlation). This is rare and highly desirable for diversification.

Key Takeaways
  • Beta measures a stock's volatility relative to the market.
  • Beta = Covariance(stock, market) ÷ Variance(market).
  • Beta > 1 is more volatile; beta < 1 is less volatile than the market.
  • The market itself has a beta of exactly 1.

What Is Beta?

Beta is a measure of how sensitive a stock's returns are to movements in the broader market. A beta of 1.5 means the stock tends to rise or fall 1.5 times as much as the market. It quantifies systematic risk — the risk you can't diversify away — and feeds directly into the cost-of-equity calculation used in valuation.

How Is Beta Calculated?

Beta equals the covariance between the stock's returns and the market's returns, divided by the variance of the market's returns. Covariance captures how the two move together; variance measures the market's own swings. For a covariance of 0.0055 and market variance of 0.0040, beta = 0.0055 ÷ 0.0040 = 1.375. The calculator performs this division instantly.

What Does the Beta Value Tell You?

A beta above 1 signals a stock more volatile than the market — higher potential reward and risk. A beta below 1 indicates a defensive stock that moves less. A negative beta, rare, means the stock tends to move opposite the market. Investors use this calculation to build portfolios matching their risk appetite.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a beta of 1 mean?

The stock moves in line with the market. A 10% market gain implies roughly a 10% gain in the stock. The market index itself has a beta of 1.

Is a high beta good or bad?

Neither inherently. High beta offers larger gains in rising markets but steeper losses in falling ones. It suits aggressive investors; low beta suits conservative ones.

Where is beta used?

In the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) to estimate expected return, and in portfolio construction to manage overall risk exposure.
