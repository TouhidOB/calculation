Compound Interest Calculator: How Does Your Money Grow Over Time?

Compound interest is often called the eighth wonder of the world — your money earns returns, and those returns earn returns, snowballing over time. Key Takeaways
  • Compound interest earns returns on both principal and prior interest.
  • Future value = P(1 + r/n)^(nt) plus the growth of contributions.
  • Time is the most powerful factor — earlier money compounds longer.
  • More frequent compounding modestly increases the final value.

What Is Compound Interest?

Compound interest is interest calculated on your initial principal plus all previously accumulated interest. Unlike simple interest, which only ever earns on the original amount, compounding lets your earnings generate their own earnings. This snowball effect is the engine of long-term investing, and understanding the calculation is key to building wealth.

How Is Compound Interest Calculated?

The core formula is A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt), where P is principal, r the annual rate, n the compounding periods per year, and t the years. The calculator also adds the future value of any regular contributions, each compounding from the moment it's deposited. Together these produce your projected total in one calculation.

Why Does Starting Early Matter?

Time is compounding's greatest ally. A dollar invested at 25 compounds for decades longer than one invested at 45, often ending up worth several times more. The calculator demonstrates this vividly — small contributions made early routinely outgrow larger ones made late, proving the power of the calculation.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between simple and compound interest?

Simple interest earns only on the principal. Compound interest earns on principal plus accumulated interest, growing much faster over time.

How does compounding frequency affect growth?

More frequent compounding (daily vs. annually) yields slightly more, because interest is added to the balance sooner and starts earning faster.

What is the rule of 72?

A shortcut: divide 72 by the annual rate to estimate the years for money to double. At 8%, money doubles in about 9 years.
