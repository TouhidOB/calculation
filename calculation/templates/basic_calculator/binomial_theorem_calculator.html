What Is the Binomial Theorem?

The binomial theorem expands any power of a sum (x+y)ⁿ into a sum of terms without multiplying the binomial repeatedly. Each term follows C(n,k)·x^(n−k)·y^k, where the coefficient counts combinations of n items taken k at a time. For n=2 it gives the familiar (x+y)² = x² + 2xy + y².

Where Do the Coefficients Come From?

Binomial coefficients come from Pascal's triangle, where each number is the sum of the two above it. Row n gives exactly the coefficients for (x+y)ⁿ. Each equals C(n, k), so the middle term of row 4 is C(4, 2) = 6. The calculator evaluates these combinations directly through factorial calculation.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many terms does (x+y)^n have?

Exactly n+1 terms. (x+y)⁵ produces six terms; (x+y)¹⁰ produces eleven. The calculator generates all of them so you never miscount.

What is a binomial coefficient?

C(n, k), read "n choose k," counts ways to pick k items from n. In the expansion it's the numerical multiplier on each term, matching Pascal's triangle.

Does the order of x and y matter?

The theorem is symmetric: x powers descend while y powers ascend. Swapping them reverses term order, but coefficients stay identical since C(n, k) = C(n, n−k).
