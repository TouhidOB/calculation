What Is a Prime Number?

A prime number is a whole number greater than 1 that has no divisors other than 1 and itself. So 7 is prime because nothing between 2 and 6 divides it evenly, while 8 is composite because 2 and 4 do. Primes are the indivisible atoms of arithmetic — every integer factors uniquely into primes, a fact central to number-theory calculation.

How Does the Checker Test Primality?

It tries to divide the number by integers up to its square root — if none divide evenly, the number is prime. Checking only to √n is the key optimization: any factor larger than the square root pairs with one smaller, so testing beyond it is redundant. This makes the calculation fast even for large inputs.

Why Are Prime Numbers Important?

Primes are the backbone of cryptography. RSA encryption, which secures online banking and messaging, relies on the difficulty of factoring the product of two large primes. Primes also appear in hashing, random number generation, and pure mathematics. Identifying them quickly powers these security calculations.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is 1 a prime number?

No. By definition a prime has exactly two distinct factors, but 1 has only one (itself). So 1 is neither prime nor composite.

Why is 2 the only even prime?

Every other even number is divisible by 2, giving it a third factor and making it composite. 2 itself has only 1 and 2 as factors, so it's prime.

Are there infinitely many primes?

Yes. Euclid proved over 2000 years ago that primes never run out — there is no largest prime number.
