What Is a Quadratic Equation?

A quadratic equation is a second-degree polynomial equation, ax² + bx + c = 0, where a is not zero. Its graph is a parabola, and its solutions — called roots — are where the parabola crosses the x-axis. Quadratics appear throughout physics, engineering, and finance, making their calculation a core algebra skill.

What Is the Quadratic Formula?

The quadratic formula, x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) ÷ 2a, solves any quadratic regardless of whether it factors neatly. The ± sign yields two roots. For x² − 5x + 6 = 0: the formula gives x = (5 ± √1) ÷ 2 = 3 or 2. The solver plugs your coefficients into this formula and returns both roots in one calculation.

What Does the Discriminant Tell You?

The discriminant, b² − 4ac, reveals the nature of the roots before you finish solving. If it's positive, there are two distinct real roots; if zero, one repeated real root; if negative, two complex roots. The solver reads the discriminant and returns complex roots when needed, so no equation is left unsolved.

Frequently Asked Questions

When are the roots complex?

When the discriminant b² − 4ac is negative, the square root involves an imaginary number, producing two complex conjugate roots. The parabola never crosses the x-axis.

Can I always use the quadratic formula?

Yes. Unlike factoring, the formula solves every quadratic, including those with irrational or complex roots. It's the universal method.

What if a equals zero?

Then it's not a quadratic but a linear equation, bx + c = 0, with a single solution x = −c/b. A quadratic requires a nonzero leading coefficient.
